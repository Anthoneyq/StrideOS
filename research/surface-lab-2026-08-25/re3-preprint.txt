22    Abstract (150-250 words)
23    Purpose: To extend the Running Energy Expenditure Estimation (RE3) model for predicting
24    metabolic rates during uphill and downhill running as well as to enhance the Hoogkamer-
25    Taboga-Kram (HTK) equation for estimating metabolic rates during level and uphill running.
26    Methods: We combined running metabolic data from an original dataset (n=63) with individual
27    subject data from 26 studies (n=424) and group mean data from 12 studies (n=187). Using this
28    integrated dataset, we derived a new graded running term for the empirical RE3 model and
29    updated the HTK equation coefficients for level and uphill running. We then compared the
30    accuracy and precision of these new equations with the established American College of Sports
31    Medicine (ACSM) and Minetti et al. equations based on the root-mean-square deviation
32    (RMSD).
33    Results: Accuracy and precision of estimating level Ṁ were high for the Minetti et al. (RMSD,
34    1.44 W∙kg-1), HTK (1.30 W∙kg-1), and RE3 (1.27 W∙kg-1) equations but much worse for the
35    ACSM equation (1.82 W∙kg-1). Agreement on uphill slopes was highest for the HTK (RMSD,
36    1.45 W∙kg-1) and RE3 (1.41 W∙kg-1) equations with less precision noted for the ACSM (2.17
37    W∙kg-1) and Minetti et al. (2.18 W∙kg-1) equations. When estimating Ṁ during downhill running,
38    the RE3 equation performed marginally better (RMSD, 1.45 W∙kg-1) than the Minetti et al.
39    equation (1.57 W∙kg-1).
40    Conclusion: The improved RE3 and HTK equations estimate metabolic rates during level and
41    graded running with improved accuracy and precision. We provide a publicly available web-
42    based metabolic rate calculator that simplifies estimation for researchers, practitioners, and
43    runners alike.

 72    Introduction
 73    Many people who run for health and fitness strive to maximize their rates of “burning calories” as
 74    an aid to weight management (Caudwell et al., 2014). In contrast, competitive runners seek to
 75    minimize their rate of metabolic energy expenditure (Ṁ) so that they can run faster at their
 76    physiological limits (Kipp et al., 2019). Military personnel and ultramarathon runners need to
 77    estimate their Ṁ so that they can consume adequate amounts of food for optimal performance
 78    during missions or races respectively. Many equations exist that can predict Ṁ during level
 79    running (Hall et al., 2004; Looney et al., 2023; Rubenson et al., 2007) and thus provide
 80    information relevant to these needs. Notably, we and our colleagues recently developed a
 81    generalized equation for estimating Ṁ during level running for the Running Energy Expenditure
 82    Estimation (RE3) model based on data compiled from over 50 studies (Looney et al., 2023).
 83    However, many runners also traverse a variety of uphill and downhill terrain. As any runner
 84    knows, uphill running is more strenuous, and downhill running is less so. But there are fewer
 85    equations that predict how much greater Ṁ is during uphill running and even fewer equations for
 86    how much Ṁ changes during downhill running.
 87              Since 1975, the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) has espoused a simple
 88    equation for predicting oxygen uptake (V̇O2) during level and uphill running (Ozemek et al.,
 89    2025). The ACSM equation assumes that V̇O2 during uphill running is equal to the sum of V̇O2
 90    for resting metabolism (3.5 mL∙kg-1∙min-1) plus the V̇O2 for running on the level and the V̇O2
 91    required to lift the body mass vertically against gravity. The third term in the equation (vertical
 92    lifting) essentially assumes an unphysiologically high efficiency value (> 50%) (see Appendix).
93     Given that isolated muscle efficiency (Barclay & Curtin, 2023) and muscular efficiency during
94     cycling exercise (Ettema & Lorås, 2009) are both about 25%, using a value > 50% is in conflict
95     with established physiology.
 96              The pioneering efforts of Minetti et al. (2002) introduced a 5th order polynomial equation
 97    based on physiological measurements that predicts the amount of metabolic energy expended
 98    during running per unit distance (J∙kg-1∙m-1, aka metabolic cost of transport, CoT) on uphill,
 99    level, and downhill slopes (referred to as the Minetti equation henceforth). However, the Minetti
100    equation neglects the costs of generating force to support body weight, brake and propel the
101    center of mass (COM) (Arellano & Kram, 2014). Further, the predictions of the Minetti equation

102    diverge substantially from metabolic data measured during very steep uphill running (Giovanelli
103    et al., 2016).
104              Hoogkamer et al. (2014) addressed the ACSM efficiency issue by accounting for
105    differences in the biomechanics between uphill and level running. The Hoogkamer et al. (2014)
106    approach (which we refer to as HTK, based on the last name initials of the authors) breaks the
107    metabolic cost of uphill running into several constituents: the cost of bouncing perpendicular to
108    the running surface (including the cost of swinging the arms and legs), the cost of running
109    parallel to the surface, and the cost of producing mechanical work to lift the center of mass
110    against gravity. At a given running speed on the level, Ṁ is directly proportional to the average
111    ground reaction force (GRF) perpendicular to the surface and inversely proportional to the time
112    of foot-ground contact (tc) (Kipp et al., 2018b; Kram & Taylor, 1990; Roberts et al., 1998). Since
113    Hoogkamer et al. (2014) found that neither the perpendicular force nor tc changed appreciably on
114    moderate inclines, and since stride frequency changes little during uphill running, they assumed
115    the combined cost of perpendicular bouncing plus limb swing remains nearly the same.
116              However, the GRF parallel to the running surface changes dramatically during uphill
117    running. The braking impulse diminishes to near zero when running up a 9° incline at which
118    slope the propulsive impulse only acts to advance the center of mass up the slope. Thus,
119    Hoogkamer et al. (2014) modelled the cost of parallel running as decreasing exponentially with
120    incline. Having accounted for the costs of generating perpendicular and parallel forces,
121    Hoogkamer et al. (2014) could then properly consider the metabolic cost of the mechanical work
122    required to lift the center of mass vertically. Fitting their equation to the metabolic data revealed
123    that a constant delta efficiency for doing vertical work (29%) was then an appropriate
124    assumption. We note that the HTK model was only developed and tested on moderate inclines (<
125    9°) and slow speeds (< 3 m∙s-1) and for a modest number of participants (n = 8). Further, the
126    HTK model predicted the metabolic cost of transport (J∙kg-1∙m-1) whereas most runners are
127    concerned with Ṁ.
128              Recently, U.S. Army researchers derived a new empirical method to precisely estimate Ṁ
129    during level, uphill, and downhill walking using a unique formula (Looney et al., 2019b). A
130    graded walking factor was incorporated into an existing level walking equation (Looney et al.,
131    2019a) and outperformed existing alternatives during cross-validation (Looney et al., 2019b).
132    Given the similar “J-shape” relationships between grade and metabolic costs of transport for both

133    walking and running (Minetti et al., 2002), deriving an empirical, graded running factor with a
134    similar formula for the RE3 model is a logical next step.
135              Our primary goal was to extend the RE3 model as per Looney et al. (2019b) to predict Ṁ
136    during level, uphill and downhill running across a range of speeds using a large database
137    aggregated from multiple sources. A secondary goal was to first transform the HTK equation
138    from CoT units to Ṁ units and then update the coefficients, using the same large database, to
139    provide a generalized equation consistent with our present understanding of level and uphill
140    running biomechanics and muscle physiology. Unfortunately, muscle function during downhill
141    running remains poorly understood, and thus we presently cannot confidently develop a
142    predictive equation for downhill running based on physiology. For ease of use, we aimed to
143    provide readers with web-based Ṁ calculator that included the newly derived methods to support
144    practical applications (University of Massachusetts Integrative Locomotion Lab., 2025).

146    Methods
147    Experimental design
148    Like our previous study (Looney et al., 2023), we combined running metabolic data from three
149    types of sources: original individual subject data, published individual subject data (Table 1), and
150    published group mean data (Table 2). We aggregated data from level, uphill, and downhill
151    running trials to derive the new graded running term of the empirical RE3 model. Data from the
152    level and uphill running trials was subsequently used to update the coefficients of the HTK
153    equation. The accuracy and precision of Ṁ estimates were subsequently compared between these
154    equations along with the existing equations from the ACSM (level and uphill running only) and
155    Minetti et al. (2002).
156

160    Level running data were previously analyzed from 45 participants from this group (Looney et al.,
161    2023). After completing a ~ 20 min warm-up walk, participants ran for 3-4 min on a motorized
162    treadmill while wearing standard physical training attire (i.e., shorts, t-shirt, socks, running
163    shoes) on two separate visits. The treadmill was initially set to approximately 70.0-77.5% of the

175    Published individual participant data
176    We searched PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar for published individual participant
177    datasets from studies that measured steady-state treadmill running metabolic energetics in
178    apparently healthy men and women (18–44 y) via indirect calorimetry. Search terms included
179    “treadmill”, “running”, “grade OR uphill OR downhill”, “energy OR metabolic OR oxygen”, and
180    “cost OR economy OR expenditure OR rate.” We reviewed the retrieved publications reference
181    lists and contacted key authors in the field to find additional studies. Authors of the current study
182    also contributed individual data they collected or had been shared with them previously. We
183    excluded data from individuals who reported conditions that would contraindicate intense
184    running exercise (cardiovascular defects, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes
185    mellitus, musculoskeletal defects or disorders, thyroid disorders, etc.). Additionally, trials were
186    excluded if they involved external loading (e.g., backpacks, weighted vests), unusual equipment
187    (e.g., biohazard protection suit, exoskeleton), environmental stressors with pronounced metabolic
188    effects (e.g., cold, heat), reported respiratory exchange ratio (RER) values less than 0.7 or greater
189    than 1.0, or reported blood lactate concentrations > 4 mmol·L-1. Table 1 displays characteristics
190    of the 424 participants (58 women) from the 26 individual participant data studies.

199    Running Energy Expenditure Estimation (RE3) Model
200    The level running equation for the RE3 model can be written as:
201    Ṁ = 4.43 + 1.51∙S + 0.37∙S2                                                                                                   Eq. 1
202    where Ṁ is rate of metabolic energy expenditure (W∙kg-1) and S is running speed (m∙s-1). We
203    sought to add an empirical graded term to Eq. 1 in order to model the “J-shaped” relationship
204    between surface grade and Ṁ (Looney et al., 2019b). Critical characteristics of this “J-shaped”
205    relationship include the increase Ṁ on positive slopes (Dill, 1965) that remains linear even at
206    extreme uphill grades (Giovanelli et al., 2016); the decrease in Ṁ on gradual downhill grades as
207    propulsive force requirements are supplied by gravity (Gottschall & Kram, 2005; Margaria et al.,
208    1963); the minimization of running Ṁ between −10 to −20% grade (Lemire et al., 2023;
209    Margaria, 1938; Minetti et al., 2002); and the eventual upturn in Ṁ at steeper negative grades
210    presumably due to the additional eccentric braking forces required to maintain a constant running
211    speed against the greater parallel component of gravitational force (Davies et al., 1974; Margaria,
212    1938; Minetti et al., 2002). The RE3 Graded Running Equation for estimating Ṁ from running

237    Statistical analysis
238    We used R Version 4.12; R Foundation for Statistical Computing; Vienna, Austria) (R Core
239    Team, 2014) to analyze all data and create each figure. Data are reported as mean ± SD unless
240    stated otherwise. Rates of metabolic energy expenditure were determined from measured V̇CO2
241    and V̇O2 with an indirect calorimetry equation (Kipp et al., 2018a) derived from a previously
242    developed nonprotein respiratory quotient table (Péronnet & Massicotte, 1991). Missing
243    individual RER values were assumed to be equal to the trial mean if reported or 0.90 when no
244    value was reported. Outliers were screened quantitatively using the 3 median absolute deviations
245    from the median approach (Leys et al., 2013).
246              Equation coefficients were determined using nonlinear mixed effects modeling with the
247    “nlme” package (Pinheiro et al., 2017) with random effects of group within study to account for
248    the dependence among repeated measures. Datapoints were weighted in the regression by n size,
249    with each individual participant coded as n = 1. We used linear mixed effects models, derived
250    using the “lme4” package (Bates et al., 2014), to assess as accuracy and precision of Ṁ estimates
251    using similar statistical procedures as our previous study (Looney et al., 2023). Accuracy was
252    assessed as the bias weighted by n size and calculated as the mean paired difference between
253    estimated and measured Ṁ. Precision was assessed as the SD of paired differences weighted by n
254    size and calculated as the root-sum of the mean squared difference between estimated Ṁ and the
255    overall bias plus the within-group variance. The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), as an

276    Discussion
277    Using a large dataset compiled from 26 studies with individual participant data and 12 studies
278    reporting group mean data, we fit a new graded running term for the RE3 model that enables Ṁ
279    estimation for level, uphill, and downhill running. Using this same large, compiled dataset, we fit
280    an updated formula for the physiologically-based HTK equation for level and uphill running.
281    Both methods proved to be accurate and precise estimators of running energetics on various
282    slopes with demonstrated improvements over two of the most used estimation equations.
283    Consequently, we provide a publicly available web-based metabolic rate calculator that
284    simplifies estimation for researchers, practitioners, and runners alike (University of
285    Massachusetts Integrative Locomotion Lab., 2025).

287              As can be seen in Fig3, on steeper inclines, the ACSM equation underestimates Ṁ
288    whereas the Minetti equation overestimates Ṁ. Perhaps this point can be best illustrated by
289    considering an athlete running in a vertical kilometer (VK) race. The official VK record for is 28
290    min 53 s set in Fully, Switzerland (Vertical Kilometer World Circuit., 2025). That course ascends
291    1,000 m over a distance of 1,920 m equating to an average grade of 0.52 and an average running
292    speed of 1.11 m∙s-1. The ACSM equation predicts Ṁ to be 16.99 W∙kg-1 whereas the Minetti
293    equation predicts 27.92 W∙kg-1, a nearly two-fold range. Whereas the RE3 model (25.51 W∙kg-1)
294    and new HTK model (24.13 W∙kg-1) predict similar intermediate Ṁ values.
295              The novel graded running term developed for the RE3 model allows for estimating Ṁ
296    across an extreme range of downhill and uphill grades (−0.45 to 0.82), the largest range of
297    surface grades that any equation has been developed on to date. This is useful not only for
298    applications such as mountain running (Balducci et al., 2016; Vernillo et al., 2020) but also for
299    team field sports where the energy cost of accelerated running has been estimated based on the
300    energy cost of running of an “equivalent slope”, or a slope with a comparable angle of forward
301    body lean (Osgnach et al., 2010). For the RE3 model to be most applicable to military
302    populations, there is a need to characterize the metabolic effects of the loads added by body
303    armor or utility vests (Looney et al., 2024), backpacks/rucksacks (Looney et al., 2022), military
304    footwear such as combat boots (Lavoie et al., 2023), and other operationally-relevant equipment
305    (Soucy et al., 2023) that servicemembers may be required to carry while running or sprinting.
306              Hoogkamer et al. (2014) expanded the cost of generating force hypothesis for level
307    running to uphill running and observed efficiency values for producing work to vertically lift the
308    center of mass only slightly higher than physiologically realistic values. However, the accuracy
309    of the original HTK model beyond the speeds (up to 3.0 m∙s-1) and the inclines (up to 8°, 0.14
310    grade) measured to fit the equation had not been assessed. By fitting the HTK equation to a much
311    larger data set including faster running speeds and steeper grades, we were able to update the
312    equation within the original biomechanical framework. The best fit resulted in an efficiency
313    value for producing work to vertically lift the center of mass of 23.0%, which is physiologically
314    realistic, more so than the 29.4% of the original equation. Intriguingly, the accuracy and
315    precision of this updated equation is similar to that of the phenomenological RE3 equation. As
316    such the updated HTK model provides a framework linking running biomechanics and energetics

318    standing Ṁ and efficiency, and it can be further expanded to include downhill running and load
319    carriage, all based on biomechanical and muscle physiological theory.
320              As they are designed for simple, generalized applications, the HTK and RE3 equations
321    estimate Ṁ for an average individual and do not account for all the variability in mass-specific
322    running economy across the spectrum of runners (Daniels, 1985). Thus, erring on the side of
323    overconsuming calories for competitive, recreational, or tactical athletes during an event would
324    be prudent. While running Ṁ can be individualized based on key aerobic fitness parameters,
325    there are many to choose from and most can only be measured accurately using laboratory
326    equipment that can be difficult for many runners to access. Additionally, these aerobic fitness
327    parameters do not perfectly correlate with inter-individual variability in Ṁ as even trained
328    runners with similar maximal oxygen uptake (V̇O2max) have been shown to vary in running
329    economy by upwards of 30% (Barnes & Kilding, 2015; Daniels, 1985). Furthermore, running
330    economy does not always translate between different conditions, such as running on the level
331    versus steep slopes (Lemire et al., 2021), highlighting the importance of exercise familiarity and
332    training experience. Developing an easy-to-use method for calculating individualized Ṁ
333    estimates that can accommodate a variety of aerobic fitness parameters as well as account for
334    experience and familiarity with specific types of running is a difficult but important aim for
335    future research.
336              While Vernillo et al. (2017) provide a concise summary of what is known about the
337    biomechanics and physiology of downhill running, developing a mechanistic-based equation for
338    downhill running will require a deeper understanding especially for grades steeper than 15%
339    (−9°). Even just quantifying the relevant metabolic rate for downhill running is not
340    straightforward because V̇O2 (and presumably Ṁ) gradually increases by as much as 10% over
341    trial durations of 40-45 min down −10% grades (Dick & Cavanagh, 1987; Westerlind et al.,
342    1994). Westerlind et al. (1994) found that in a second downhill running trial, initial V̇O2 was ~
343    8.5% lower than the initial value in the first trial but then increased again (by ~ 8%) over the
344    course of the second 40 min trial. Note that Dick and Cavanagh (1987) studied experienced
345    runners whereas Westerlind et al. (1994) intentionally studied individuals who were not
346    accustomed to downhill running. Future research is needed to determine how leg muscle
347    fascicles actually behave during downhill running beyond the usual assumption of eccentric

356    Conclusion
357    Our analysis provides researchers, practitioners, and runners with the capability to estimate Ṁ
358    during running on level, uphill, and downhill slopes using the novel graded running term fit for
359    the existing RE3 model. The newly developed web browser-based metabolic rate calculator
360    (University of Massachusetts Integrative Locomotion Lab., 2025) provides users with a simple
361    way to calculate Ṁ with the updated RE3 model. Additionally, the enhanced, generalized version
362    of the HTK equation provides close estimates of Ṁ during level and uphill running that is
363    consistent with present understanding of level and uphill running biomechanics and muscle
364    physiology.

365    Appendix 1 Deriving the efficiency value assumed by the American College of Sports Medicine
366    (ACSM) running equation.
367
368    The ACSM equation for estimating oxygen uptake (V̇O2) during running is:
369    V̇O2 (mL∙kg-1∙min-1) = 3.5 + (0.2∙Speed) + (0.9∙Speed∙Grade)                                                                   Eq. 6
370    where speed is in units of m/min and grade is expressed as a decimal percentage, i.e. 10% grade
371    is 0.10. The vertical mechanical power (work rate) in watts (W) is equal to the product of body
372    mass, gravitational acceleration (9.81 m/s2), treadmill belt velocity (in m/s) and the sin of the
373    incline angle (θ). For example, the vertical mechanical power for a 70 kg person running at 180
374    m/min up a 10% incline (θ = 5.7°) is equal to mg v sin θ or 70 kg∙9.81 m∙s-2∙3 m∙s-1∙sin 5.7°
375    which is equal to 204 W. The corresponding V̇O2 from the third term in the ACSM equation for
376    is 0.9∙180∙0.10 or 16.2 mL O2/kg/min. Multiplying 16.2 by body mass (70 kg) and dividing by 60
377    s per min yields 18.9 mL O2∙s-1 which converts to a rate of metabolic energy expenditure (Ṁ) of
378    395 W, assuming 20.9 J/mL O2 (Gill et al. 2023). Efficiency is the ratio of mechanical power and
379    Ṁ which, in this example, is 204 W/395 W or 51.9%.
