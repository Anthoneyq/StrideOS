# Source Notes — Running Surface Equivalency

## 1. Minetti et al. (2002): extreme uphill and downhill energy cost

- **Source:** Alberto E. Minetti, Christian Moia, Giulio S. Roi, Davide Susta, and Guido Ferretti, “Energy cost of walking and running at extreme uphill and downhill slopes,” *Journal of Applied Physiology* 93(3), 1039–1046. DOI: [10.1152/japplphysiol.01177.2001](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.01177.2001). PubMed: [PMID 12183501](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12183501/).
- **Design:** Ten runners; treadmill gradients from −0.45 to +0.45 (dimensionless rise/run, equivalent to −45% through +45%) at different speeds.
- **Outcome definition:** Running energy cost, `Cr`, in J·kg⁻¹·m⁻¹.
- **Reported points:** Level `Cr = 3.40 ± 0.24 J·kg⁻¹·m⁻¹`, stated to be independent of speed in the tested level-running range. At +45%, `Cr = 18.93 ± 1.74`. At −20%, the reported minimum was `1.73 ± 0.36`; at −45% cost rose again to `3.92 ± 0.81`.
- **Interpretation:** Uphill cost increases steeply with positive grade. Moderate downhill running lowers metabolic cost per metre, but very steep negative grades increase it again because eccentric braking and mechanical demands rise.
- **Scope warning:** The widely reproduced polynomial needs confirmation from the full text, not just the PubMed abstract. It is a steady-state metabolic model measured on a treadmill, not a direct sprint-time conversion.

## 2. Shahidi et al. (2026): overground versus 1% treadmill

- **Source:** Seyed Houtan Shahidi, Rana Can, Furkan Murat Paça, and Muhammed Doğukan Zengin, “Overground running incurs a higher energetic cost than treadmill running at a 1% grade: A comparison of running economy, oxygen cost of transport, and energy cost in endurance athletes,” *PLOS One*. DOI: [10.1371/journal.pone.0355988](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0355988).
- **Design:** Twelve trained male endurance runners (21.3 ± 2.4 y); identical 3-minute stages from 8–15 km·h⁻¹ on a 400 m outdoor track and an indoor treadmill set at 1%. Intensities were scaled to ventilatory thresholds; breath-by-breath gas exchange was averaged over the final 60–90 s of each stage.
- **Results:** Overground running economy measure was 51.3 ± 2.2 vs 49.6 ± 3.1 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹. Oxygen cost of transport was 204.2 ± 11.3 vs 197.1 ± 12.3 mL·kg⁻¹·km⁻¹. Energy cost was higher overground across the reported range: 3.87 ± 0.21 to 4.60 ± 0.27 versus 3.65 ± 0.22 to 4.10 ± 0.18 J·kg⁻¹·m⁻¹. The paper reports statistically significant condition effects.
- **Interpretation:** In this sample and protocol, the classic 1% treadmill adjustment did **not** fully reproduce outdoor-track energetic demand.
- **Scope warning:** Small, male-only sample; track and indoor environmental conditions differ; results should update but not erase earlier speed-specific findings. A single universal treadmill correction is not defensible from this evidence.

## Research rules established

1. Keep **grade geometry**, **steady-state metabolic cost**, **aerodynamic power**, **mechanical power**, **sprint acceleration**, and **muscle-tissue loading** as separate layers.
2. Do not call two sessions “equivalent” without naming the conserved quantity: elapsed time, horizontal distance, path distance, vertical gain, external work, metabolic energy, average metabolic power, or perceived effort.
3. Present any calculator as a model-based estimate with a validity range, not as a physiological diagnosis or guaranteed performance conversion.

## 3. Jones & Doust (1996): origin and limits of the 1% rule

- **Source:** Andrew M. Jones and Jonathan H. Doust, “A 1% treadmill grade most accurately reflects the energetic cost of outdoor running,” *Journal of Sports Sciences* 14(4), 321–327. DOI: [10.1080/02640419608727717](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640419608727717). PubMed: [PMID 8887211](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8887211/).
- **Design:** Nine trained, treadmill-habituated men ran 6-minute bouts at 2.92, 3.33, 3.75, 4.17, 4.58, and 5.00 m·s⁻¹. The protocol was repeated on treadmill grades of 0%, 1%, 2%, and 3% (with a repeated 0% condition) and on a level outdoor road. Expired air was collected during the final two minutes.
- **Results:** At 2.92 and 3.33 m·s⁻¹, road oxygen uptake was not significantly different from either 0% or 1% treadmill running. At 3.75 m·s⁻¹, road running differed from 0%, 2%, and 3%, but not 1%. At 4.17 and 4.58 m·s⁻¹, road running was not significantly different from 1% or 2%. At 5.00 m·s⁻¹, the road value fell between 1% and 2%, but the study did not detect a significant difference from any treadmill grade.
- **Interpretation:** The paper supports 1% as a practical approximation for approximately five minutes of running within the studied speed range, but not as an exact physical law. The slower two speeds did not require the correction statistically, and the faster speeds were also compatible with 2% within the study’s precision.
- **Scope warning:** Nine male runners, one treadmill/road setup, oxygen uptake as the primary comparison, no downhill conditions, and no sprint-duration trials. Belt calibration, deck mechanics, ambient airflow, temperature, turns, and athlete familiarity can alter the result.

## 4. Van Hooren et al. (2020): biomechanical treadmill–overground comparison

- **Source:** Bas Van Hooren et al., “Is Motorized Treadmill Running Biomechanically Comparable to Overground Running? A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Cross-Over Studies,” *Sports Medicine* 50, 785–813. DOI: [10.1007/s40279-019-01237-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01237-z). PubMed: [PMID 31802395](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31802395/).
- **Scope:** 33 studies and 494 participants. The eligibility framing emphasized non-inclined, non-cushioned, quasi-constant-velocity motorized treadmill running versus overground running in healthy adults.
- **Main result:** Most measured outcomes did not differ, so the two modes were **largely**, not perfectly, biomechanically comparable.
- **Pooled treadmill differences:** Sagittal foot–ground angle at footstrike was 9.8° lower (95% CI −13.1 to −6.6); knee-flexion range from footstrike to peak stance was 6.3° lower; vertical centre-of-mass/pelvis displacement was 1.5 cm lower; peak propulsive force was 0.04 body weights lower; contact time was 5.0 ms longer. Evidence ratings for these effects ranged from low to very low. Muscle-activity findings conflicted.
- **Interpretation:** Matching average metabolic demand does not guarantee identical footstrike geometry, propulsive force, contact time, or tissue loading. Treadmill-deck properties and protocol details matter.

## 5. Miller et al. (2019): physiology, perception, and performance meta-analysis

- **Source:** Jayme R. Miller et al., “A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Crossover Studies Comparing Physiological, Perceptual and Performance Measures Between Treadmill and Overground Running,” *Sports Medicine* 49, 763–782. DOI: [10.1007/s40279-019-01087-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01087-9). PubMed: [PMID 30847825](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30847825/).
- **Scope:** 34 crossover studies. Twelve used a 1% treadmill grade and three used grades above 1%.
- **Submaximal findings:** Oxygen uptake was similar between motorized treadmill and overground running at both 0% (mean difference −0.55 ± 0.93 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹) and 1% (0.37 ± 1.12). Blood lactate was lower on the treadmill at 0% (−1.26 ± 0.71 mmol·L⁻¹) and 1% (−0.52 ± 0.50). Heart rate and RPE were higher on the treadmill at faster submaximal speeds but lower at slower speeds.
- **Near-maximal and maximal findings:** Near-maximal treadmill oxygen uptake and lactate tended to be lower, while heart rate and RPE were similar. At maximal intensity, oxygen uptake and heart rate were similar.
- **Performance:** Endurance performance was poorer on motorized treadmills (standardized mean difference −0.50 ± 0.36). Sprint performance varied considerably and did not differ significantly overall (mean difference −1.4 ± 5.8 km·h⁻¹).
- **Interpretation:** Equivalence depends on **which outcome and speed** is being matched. Oxygen uptake, blood lactate, heart rate, perceived exertion, endurance performance, and sprint speed cannot be replaced by one universal conversion factor.

## 6. di Prampero et al. (2005): acceleration as an “equivalent slope”

- **Source:** P. E. di Prampero, S. Fusi, L. Sepulcri, J. B. Morin, A. Belli, and G. Antonutto, “Sprint running: a new energetic approach,” *Journal of Experimental Biology* 208(14), 2809–2816. DOI: [10.1242/jeb.01700](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.01700). PubMed: [PMID 16000549](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16000549/).
- **Design:** Twelve medium-level male sprinters performed an all-out 30 m sprint from rest on a flat track while radar measured speed. Mean peak speed was 9.46 ± 0.19 m·s⁻¹ after about 5 s; maximum forward acceleration immediately after the start was 6.42 ± 0.61 m·s⁻².
- **Geometry:** With `g` as gravitational acceleration and `a_f` as forward acceleration, the body-axis angle is `α = arctan(g/a_f)`. The equivalent uphill grade is `ES = tan(90° − α)`, which simplifies to `ES = a_f/g`. The reported maximum was `0.643 ± 0.059` (about 64.3%).
- **Model output:** The authors used literature data for constant-speed uphill cost to estimate sprint cost. Peak estimated sprint cost was 43.8 ± 10.4 J·kg⁻¹·m⁻¹; mean cost over 30 m was 10.7 ± 0.59 versus 3.8 for constant-speed level running. Estimated peak and mean metabolic powers were 91.9 ± 20.5 and 61.0 ± 4.7 W·kg⁻¹.
- **Interpretation:** Acceleration creates a fore–aft inertial demand analogous, in the model, to gravity’s tangential component on an incline. This is useful for decomposing sprint effort but does **not** mean a stationary treadmill at a 64% grade replicates a maximal track start in coordination, velocity, contact mechanics, or muscle action.
- **Scope warning:** The energetic values are indirect estimates built by mapping transient acceleration onto steady-speed uphill data. Later criticism identifies sensitivity to data smoothing, instantaneous acceleration estimates, assumed starting speed, air drag, and the treatment of internal mechanical work. The site must label this as a model, not a measured oxygen-cost equivalence.

The journal’s normal page presented a captcha, but its passive text extraction exposed the citation, summary, and PDF link. No attempt was made to bypass the control. The equation details should be verified from the article’s accessible PDF or an independent scholarly treatment.

### Exact equations verified from the full article PDF

The full PDF confirms the following model. For forward acceleration `a_f` and gravitational acceleration `g`:

`g′ = √(a_f² + g²)`

`α = arctan(g/a_f)`

`ES = tan(90° − α) = a_f/g`

`EM = g′/g = √(1 + a_f²/g²)`

For terrain grade `x` expressed as a decimal ratio, the Minetti constant-speed cost polynomial reproduced by di Prampero et al. is:

`C(x) = 155.4x⁵ − 30.4x⁴ − 43.3x³ + 46.3x² + 19.5x + 3.6` J·kg⁻¹·m⁻¹.

The sprint estimate is:

`C_sr = C(ES) · EM`

and instantaneous net metabolic power is:

`P_met = C_sr · v` W·kg⁻¹, because `(J·kg⁻¹·m⁻¹)(m·s⁻¹) = W·kg⁻¹`.

The paper fits sprint speed with `v(t) = v_max(1 − e^(−t/τ))`, differentiates it to obtain `a_f(t) = (v_max/τ)e^(−t/τ)`, and integrates it to obtain distance `d(t) = v_max[t − τ(1 − e^(−t/τ))]`.

The paper explicitly states major assumptions: the runner’s mass is treated as concentrated at the centre of mass; limb-motion/internal-work differences between sprinting and uphill running are neglected; landing mechanics are assumed comparable at equal equivalent slopes; the +45% Minetti measurement range was extrapolated to peak equivalent slopes near +70% for only the first few metres; and aerobic steady-state uphill measurements are used to express predominantly anaerobic sprint requirements in oxygen-equivalent energy units. The reported sprint costs and powers are therefore indirect estimates and may be lower bounds when internal limb work is greater.

## 7. Lejeune et al. (1998): deformable sand versus hard ground

- **Source:** T. M. Lejeune, P. A. Willems, and N. C. Heglund, “Mechanics and energetics of human locomotion on sand,” *Journal of Experimental Biology* 201(13), 2071–2080. DOI: [10.1242/jeb.201.13.2071](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.201.13.2071). PubMed: [PMID 9622579](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9622579/).
- **Method:** Force-platform and cinematographic analysis measured mechanical work; oxygen consumption measured energetic cost during walking and running on sand versus a hard surface at matched speed.
- **Running result:** Sand required about **1.15×** the external mechanical work but **1.6×** the metabolic energy expenditure of hard-surface running at the same speed.
- **Mechanism:** Energy is spent deforming the sand, and positive work by the muscle–tendon system becomes less efficient because elastic energy return is reduced.
- **Model use:** A study-specific surface multiplier can be written `E_sand ≈ 1.6 E_firm` for comparable conditions, but it is not a universal “sand grade.” Sand depth, moisture, compaction, footwear, and speed can materially change the ratio. It should never be folded into the Minetti grade polynomial as if substrate loss were gravitational work.

## 8. Moon et al. (2025): track, asphalt, grass, and treadmill biomechanics

- **Source:** Griffin R. Moon, Brian K. Leary, Anh-Dung Nguyen, and Jean L. McCrory, “A comparison of running biomechanics on track, asphalt, grass, and treadmill using wearable sensors,” *Sports Biomechanics* 24(9), 2766–2778. DOI: [10.1080/14763141.2025.2511766](https://doi.org/10.1080/14763141.2025.2511766). PubMed: [PMID 40434032](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40434032/).
- **Method:** Twenty healthy participants ran at a controlled self-selected speed on asphalt, synthetic track, grass, and treadmill. Thirty steps per surface were analyzed using tibial and shoe-mounted inertial sensors.
- **Results:** Peak tibial acceleration was 11.0 ± 2.7 g on asphalt, 10.7 ± 3.4 g on grass, 10.6 ± 2.3 g on track, and 7.9 ± 1.6 g on treadmill. Rearfoot eversion velocity was lowest on grass (394.9 ± 256.5°·s⁻¹) and highest on track (623.5 ± 299.3) and asphalt (620.7 ± 289.1). Stride frequency differed, while stance time did not.
- **Interpretation:** Even when speed is controlled, common flat surfaces can change impact acceleration and foot-motion outcomes. The study did **not** measure metabolic cost, so these numbers must not be converted into calorie or distance multipliers.
- **Data-quality note:** The PubMed text reports treadmill stride frequency as “83.4 ± 5.4 strides/sec” while grass is listed as 81.0 ± 4.2 strides/min; the first unit is almost certainly a transcription/unit inconsistency and will not be reproduced as a literal per-second value without checking the article.

## 9. Lu et al. (2025): current graded-running review

- **Source:** Zeyu Lu et al., “A review of uphill and downhill running: biomechanics, physiology and modulating factors,” *Frontiers in Bioengineering and Biotechnology* 13. DOI: [10.3389/fbioe.2025.1690023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fbioe.2025.1690023).
- **Synthesis:** Uphill running generally reduces the normal impact peak while increasing parallel propulsive demand; downhill running increases normal impact and parallel braking demand. Increasing uphill slope tends to shift runners toward mid/forefoot contacts, while negative slope increases rearfoot-strike prevalence. Uphill locomotion relies on increased positive joint work and metabolic energy; downhill locomotion relies heavily on eccentric braking, so lower oxygen cost must not be read as lower musculoskeletal stress.
- **Practical implication:** A chart comparing metabolic cost alone should include a separate tissue-loading axis. “Cheaper per metre” on a moderate decline and “easier recovery” are not synonyms.
- **Evidence note:** This is a narrative review and should be used to connect primary studies, not to manufacture pooled effect sizes. Its quantitative force claims below were checked against Gottschall and Kram (2005).

## 10. Gottschall & Kram (2005): ground-reaction forces across ±3°, ±6°, and ±9°

- **Source:** Jinger S. Gottschall and Rodger Kram, “Ground reaction forces during downhill and uphill running,” *Journal of Biomechanics* 38(3), 445–452. DOI: [10.1016/j.jbiomech.2004.04.023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2004.04.023). PubMed: [PMID 15652542](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15652542/).
- **Method:** Five men and five women ran at 3 m·s⁻¹ on a force treadmill at level and on ±3°, ±6°, and ±9° wedges. These angles correspond to grades of approximately ±5.2%, ±10.5%, and ±15.8% because `grade = 100 tan(θ)`.
- **Downhill result:** At −9°, normal impact-force peaks were 54% greater and parallel braking-force peaks were 73% greater than level.
- **Uphill result:** At +9°, normal impact-force peaks were absent and parallel propulsive peaks were 75% greater than level. Normal active-force peaks did not change materially uphill or downhill.
- **Interpretation:** Grade redistributes force rather than applying one scalar “hardness.” Uphill can be metabolically and propulsively demanding while reducing the impact transient; downhill can be metabolically cheaper at moderate grades while substantially increasing impact and braking.
- **Scope warning:** Ten participants, fixed 3 m·s⁻¹ speed, treadmill/wedge apparatus, and acute laboratory exposure. Force percentages at 9° are not universal predictions for every runner, pace, or surface.

## 11. Delaney et al. (2022): maximal uphill sprint load–velocity profile

- **Source:** Jace A. Delaney et al., “Uphill sprinting load- and force-velocity profiling: Assessment and potential applications,” *Journal of Sports Sciences* 40(3), 281–287. DOI: [10.1080/02640414.2021.1992868](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2021.1992868). PubMed: [PMID 34727844](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34727844/).
- **Method:** Twenty-four collegiate athletes performed maximal sprints on flat terrain and hills of 5.2%, 8.8%, and 17.6% grade. Radar captured velocity–time curves; load–velocity relationships used maximal speed in each condition.
- **Result:** The individual load–velocity relationships were highly linear (`R² = 0.99`) and reliable (typical error below 4.4%). Across the tested range, maximal sprint velocity decreased by **1.7 ± 0.1% for each additional degree of uphill angle**. Converting percent grade to degrees requires `θ = arctan(G/100)`; the coefficient is per degree, not per grade-percent.
- **Training interpretation:** Even 17.6% grade produced less than 25% velocity loss and was categorized as low resistance relative to the full load–velocity spectrum. The authors concluded these grades may support late-stage technical ability but did not provide enough resistance to optimize power development.
- **Scope warning:** This empirical linear relation describes maximal sprint velocity in this cohort and these three grades; it is not a metabolic-equivalence formula and should not be extrapolated to very steep hills, jogging, or long intervals.

## 12. Ebben et al. (2008): acute downhill overspeed across multiple slopes

- **Source:** William P. Ebben, Joseph A. Davies, and Rustin W. Clewien, “Effect of the degree of hill slope on acute downhill running velocity and acceleration,” *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* 22(3), 898–902. DOI: [10.1519/JSC.0b013e31816a4149](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e31816a4149). PubMed: [PMID 18438224](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18438224/).
- **Method:** Forty-four athletes ran 40-yard downhill sprints on five slopes from 2.1° to 6.9°, with 10-yard splits measured by timing gates.
- **Result:** Approximately **5.8° downhill** was optimal among the tested slopes. It increased maximal speed by `7.09% ± 3.66%` and acceleration by `6.54% ± 1.56%` versus flat running. A 5.8° descent is approximately a −10.2% grade because `100 tan(−5.8°) ≈ −10.2%`.
- **Interpretation:** A modest descent may provide acute overspeed, while steeper is not automatically faster. This is consistent with the rising braking and impact demands observed on negative grades.
- **Safety and scope warning:** Acute 40-yard sprint outcomes do not establish long-term training benefit or safety. Downhill maximal sprinting adds substantial eccentric and impact load and should not be presented as a general prescription. Exact velocity changes are cohort- and slope-specific, not a universal downhill pace calculator.

## 13. World Athletics: 400 m standard-track geometry

- **Official source:** [World Athletics Technical Information](https://worldathletics.org/about-iaaf/documents/technical-information) and the official *400 Metre Standard Track, Marking Plan* (Figure 2.2.1.6a; 2019 marking plan, with construction measurements verified in the 2008 IAAF plan).
- **Standard geometry:** Construction curve radius is 36.500 m. Lane 1’s running measurement line is 0.30 m outside the raised kerb, giving radius 36.800 m. Each straight is 84.390 m; each lane-1 bend along the measurement line is 115.611 m. Total lane-1 measurement-line distance is 400.001 m (rounding of a nominal 400 m lap). Standard lane width is 1.220 m including the outside 0.05 m line.
- **Outer-lane rule:** With the exception of lane 1, lanes are measured 0.20 m from the outer edge of their inner lane line. The official two-bend 400 m staggers from lane 1 are 7.038, 14.704, 22.370, 30.034, 37.700, 45.366, and 53.032 m for lanes 2–8.
- **Geometry formula:** For a path consisting of two straights of length `L` and two semicircles at running-line radius `r`, `D = 2L + 2πr`. A runner who ignores the stagger and completes a full lap in outer lane `n` covers approximately `D_n = 2L + 2πr_n`, where `r_n` must be computed from the appropriate measurement line, not merely `r_1 + (n−1)w` because lane 1 is measured 0.30 m from the kerb while the others are measured 0.20 m from their inner lines.
- **Simplified lane increment:** Using standard lane width `w = 1.22 m`, each successive outer lane adds approximately `2πw = 7.665 m` per full lap after accounting for the common measurement-line convention. Official staggers should be preferred for exact track work.
- **Curve mechanics:** Track geometry determines distance but not a universal effort penalty. On a flat unbanked curve, required centripetal acceleration is `a_c = v²/r` and inward force is `F_c = mv²/r`. This lateral demand changes body lean and force direction but is not a direct metabolic-cost formula. The lane-radius and speed dependence should be shown separately from grade cost.

## 14. Looney, Hoogkamer & Kram (2026): RE3 graded-running model

- **Peer-reviewed source:** David P. Looney, Wouter Hoogkamer, and Rodger Kram, “Metabolic energy expenditure during level, uphill, and downhill running,” *European Journal of Applied Physiology* 126, 1621–1633 (published online 7 October 2025; volume year 2026). DOI: [10.1007/s00421-025-05999-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-025-05999-5). PubMed: [PMID 41057734](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41057734/). An open preprint is available at [bioRxiv 10.1101/2025.06.05.658094](https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.06.05.658094).
- **Correction status:** The journal page states that a correction was published on 2 May 2026 and that the article has been updated. The final journal version, not the preprint alone, should govern coefficient values.
- **Dataset:** Original data from 62 participants, individual data from 26 studies (424 participants), and group means from 12 studies (187 participants). Included apparently healthy adults, steady-state treadmill running, indirect calorimetry, and grades spanning the compiled level/uphill/downhill literature; external loads, unusual equipment, marked environmental stress, and non-steady metabolic trials were excluded.
- **Validation comparison:** Level-running RMSD was 1.27 W·kg⁻¹ for RE3, 1.30 for updated HTK, 1.44 for Minetti, and 1.82 for ACSM. Uphill RMSD was 1.41 for RE3, 1.45 for HTK, 2.17 for ACSM, and 2.18 for Minetti. Downhill RMSD was 1.45 for RE3 versus 1.57 for Minetti.
- **Interpretation:** RE3 is the strongest practical candidate in this report for estimating **steady-state gross metabolic rate** from both speed and grade; the Minetti polynomial remains valuable because it is transparent, continuous from −45% to +45%, and directly yields cost per path metre. The site should offer both and explain the difference between a legacy cost-of-transport curve and a newer large-dataset rate model.
- **Physiology-based alternative:** The updated HTK model decomposes cost into perpendicular bouncing/limb swing, parallel force production, and vertical work. The accessible preprint text reports an updated vertical-work efficiency near 23.0%, but exact final corrected coefficients still require equation-level verification.
- **Equation access note:** The RE3 and HTK equations are rendered as embedded formula images on the open full-text page. Their metadata or corrected PDF must be inspected before implementation; coefficient values will not be inferred from prose.

### Exact equations verified from the public RE3 manuscript PDF

The public manuscript PDF was retrieved and the equation page rendered at high resolution. With running speed `S` in m·s⁻¹, decimal grade `G = rise/run`, and gross metabolic rate `Ṁ` in W·kg⁻¹, the fitted graded RE3 equation shown in the manuscript is:

`Ṁ_RE3 = 4.43 + 1.51S + 0.37S² + 30.43SG[1 − 1.133^(1 − 1.056^(100G + 43))]`.

At `G = 0`, the graded term is zero and the level model reduces to:

`Ṁ_level = 4.43 + 1.51S + 0.37S²`.

The updated HTK equation shown in the same PDF is:

`Ṁ_HTK = Ṁ_stand + S[2.01 + 1.74e^(−18.24 sinθ) + (g/0.23)sinθ]`,

where `Ṁ_stand = 1.44 W·kg⁻¹`, `g = 9.81 m·s⁻²`, and `θ = arctan(G)`. For direct computation without an angle conversion, `sinθ = G/√(1+G²)`.

Because the final journal page notes a 2026 correction, the implementation should identify these as the coefficients visually verified in the publicly available manuscript and cross-check the authors’ live calculator behavior. If the corrected final equation differs, the site must use the corrected coefficients and note the change.

For a body mass `m` in kg and duration `t` in seconds, gross metabolic energy is `E = Ṁ · m · t` joules, or `kcal = Ṁ · m · t / 4184`. For path distance `d` at constant speed, `t = d/S`. These conversions do not remove the model’s steady-state and population-level assumptions.

### Authors’ calculator check

The [UMass Integrative Locomotion Laboratory calculator](https://sites.google.com/umass.edu/umill/calculator) presents grade in decimal rise/run, speed with selectable units, output-unit selection, and separate HTK and RE3 outputs. Its calculation form is embedded in a cross-origin custom frame, so the parent page cannot inspect or automate its fields programmatically. The interface itself confirms that HTK and RE3 are intentionally presented as parallel model outputs rather than one being a hidden replacement for the other. Local implementation tests will therefore compare deterministic hand calculations and visible calculator spot checks, without circumventing iframe isolation.

## 15. Aerodynamic drag: Pugh (1970), Davies (1980), and da Silva et al. (2022)

- **Pugh source:** L. G. C. E. Pugh, “Oxygen intake in track and treadmill running with observations on the effect of air resistance,” *Journal of Physiology* 207(3), 823–835. DOI: [10.1113/jphysiol.1970.sp009097](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.1970.sp009097). PubMed: [PMID 5532903](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5532903/); open full text [PMC1348744](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1348744/).
- **Pugh findings:** Seven athletes ran on cinder/all-weather tracks and four on a treadmill. Track oxygen uptake rose more steeply with speed than treadmill oxygen uptake. In a treadmill wind experiment, additional oxygen uptake rose with wind speed squared; when relative air speed equals running speed in calm outdoor air, the **rate** of added energy use rises approximately with speed cubed. Pugh estimated air resistance at about 8% of total cost at 21.5 km·h⁻¹ (6.0 m·s⁻¹) and 16% for a 10.0 s 100 m sprint.
- **Davies source:** C. T. Davies, “Effects of wind assistance and resistance on the forward motion of a runner,” *Journal of Applied Physiology* 48(4), 702–709. DOI: [10.1152/jappl.1980.48.4.702](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1980.48.4.702). PubMed: [PMID 7380693](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7380693/).
- **Davies findings:** Three men ran with and against wind in a wind tunnel and on equivalent uphill/downhill treadmill grades. Davies estimated calm-air resistance as 7.8% of energy cost at 10 m·s⁻¹ sprint speed, 4% at 6 m·s⁻¹, and 2% at 5 m·s⁻¹. The estimates are lower than Pugh’s and illustrate model/participant uncertainty.
- **Modern source:** Edson Soares da Silva, Rodger Kram, and Wouter Hoogkamer, “The metabolic cost of emulated aerodynamic drag forces in marathon running,” *Journal of Applied Physiology* 133(3), 535–545. DOI: [10.1152/japplphysiol.00086.2022](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00086.2022).
- **Modern findings:** Twelve competitive male runners completed 5-minute treadmill bouts at 12, 14, and 16 km·h⁻¹ with 0, 4, or 8 N horizontal impeding force. Gross metabolic power increased by 6.13% per impeding force equal to 1% of body weight, with individual slopes from 4.17% to 8.14%. The response was approximately linear over the tested force range. The authors cautioned against converting drag mechanical power with generic “running efficiency.”

### Aerodynamic equations

For air density `ρ` (kg·m⁻³), effective drag area `CdA` (m²), runner ground speed `v` (m·s⁻¹), and along-course wind speed `w` defined positive as a headwind, relative air speed is `v_rel = v + w`. The drag magnitude is:

`F_drag = ½ρCdA · v_rel²`.

Mechanical power the runner must deliver against that drag while moving over ground is:

`P_drag,mech = F_drag · v = ½ρCdA · v_rel² · v`.

In calm air, `v_rel = v`, so `P_drag,mech = ½ρCdA · v³`. With a tailwind stronger than running speed, drag direction reverses; the sign must be handled using `F_drag = ½ρCdA · v_rel|v_rel|` and runner-opposing power `P = F_drag·v`.

The physical drag equations are exact idealizations, but converting mechanical drag power to **metabolic** power requires an empirically supported response. One study-specific approximation from da Silva et al. is `ΔṀ% ≈ 6.13 · 100F_drag/(mg)`, where `F_drag/(mg)` is the drag force as a fraction of body weight. Its substantial individual range and tested-speed limits must be shown. The website should therefore allow optional wind/CdA exploration but keep it separate from the core grade model and never imply that a fixed 1% treadmill incline cancels all outdoor effects.

## 16. Willwacher et al. (2021): treadmill belt speed is not perfectly constant

- **Source:** Steffen Willwacher et al., “A new method for measuring treadmill belt velocity fluctuations: effects of treadmill type, body mass and locomotion speed,” *Scientific Reports* 11, 2244. DOI: [10.1038/s41598-021-81951-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-81951-9).
- **Method:** Seven active adults walked and ran on four treadmill types. Running targets ranged from 2.5 to 4.5 m·s⁻¹. Three-dimensional motion capture tracked average and within-step belt speed.
- **Results:** Average belt speed ranged from about **98% to 103% of the displayed target** across machines and conditions. In three of four treadmills, belt-speed reduction during running stance exceeded 5% of target speed and occurred before 50% of stance. Fluctuation patterns depended on treadmill type, running speed, and body mass.
- **Interpretation:** A displayed pace is an instrument setting, not a guaranteed measured speed. A 2–3% mean speed error directly changes elapsed-time and distance calculations and can obscure small energetic differences such as a 1% grade correction.
- **Calibration formula:** If belt length is `L_belt` metres and `N` revolutions occur in measured time `t`, average belt speed is `v_belt = N·L_belt/t`. The true distance covered over duration `T` is `d = v_belt·T`. Incline should likewise be checked geometrically as `G = Δh/run`; console percent may not equal measured belt-deck rise/run.
- **Practical rule:** Research or close training comparisons should document treadmill model, measured speed, measured grade, deck condition, airflow/fan, temperature, footwear, and familiarization. The website should expose a ±3% speed-calibration sensitivity rather than treating treadmill readouts as exact.
